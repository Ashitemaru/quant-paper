\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

Our experiments address five questions. To evaluate how far current models are from a strong research agent, and how reasoning effort changes this, we compare 38 iterative configurations of nine models (Section~\ref{sec:overall}). To evaluate why predictive skill does not become profit, we analyze next-day returns by signal decile (Section~\ref{sec:signal}). To evaluate whether any strategy adapts to the market regime, we trace Astra's account and exposure (Section~\ref{sec:timing}). To evaluate what iteration contributes, we pair iterative and one-shot runs and follow candidates through the research period (Section~\ref{sec:iteration}). To evaluate whether models share a trading style, we measure their holding exposures (Section~\ref{sec:style}).

We run \ModelClaudeOpus{}, \ModelClaudeSonnet{}, \ModelAstra{}, \ModelSol{}, \ModelTerra{}, \ModelGemini{}, \ModelDeepSeek{}, \ModelKimi{}, and \ModelGLM{} in the iterative and one-shot settings at every reasoning effort they support: six levels for Terra, five for Opus, Sonnet, Astra, and Sol, four for DeepSeek, three for Gemini and Kimi, and two for GLM. This gives 38 iterative and 38 one-shot configurations. The one-shot results are in Appendix~\ref{app:oneshot}. We report the total score $S$ with its research-period and sealed-period components, and for the sealed 2024 window the annualized return (ARR), excess ARR over the gross equal-weight market, mean RankIC, long-only Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown. The diagnostics in Sections~\ref{sec:signal}--\ref{sec:style} use, for each model, the iterative configuration with the highest total score (the shaded rows of Table~\ref{tab:main}).

\noindent\textbf{Profitable strategies are rare, and only one beats the market.} Thirteen of the 38 configurations earn positive ARR, and the only one above the 17.1\% market is Sol at medium effort (18.4\%, $+1.4$ pp). Most strategies keep under half of their capital in stocks (mean exposure 34--48\% in 35 of the 38 runs), so they pay trading costs while capturing only part of the market's gain.

\noindent\textbf{Higher reasoning effort does not reliably raise the score.} For six of the nine models the best effort is not the highest one supported (Table~\ref{tab:main}): Opus scores 44.28 at medium against 41.39 at max, Gemini 43.04 against 35.39, and Kimi 38.31 at low against 31.86 at max. Only Sonnet, Terra, and GLM peak at their highest effort. The range across a model's efforts reaches 14.49 points (Terra), close to the 18.77-point range between the nine highest-effort scores. Effort acts through discrete design choices rather than gradual improvement. Astra's xhigh and max runs add a market-state filter and hold cash on 70--75\% of days, while its low, medium, and high runs stay about 47--48\% invested, and its score jumps from 43.70 at high to 51.29 at xhigh. Sol follows the same pattern: its xhigh run is 5\% invested on average against 34--46\% at its other efforts.

\subsection{Why Models Fall Short: Ranking Skill Does Not Reach the Portfolio}
\label{sec:signal}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/fig_deciles_r598.png}
\caption{Mean next-day return by daily score decile for the nine models' best iterative configurations in 2024, in basis points (bp). H--L is the highest-minus-lowest decile spread, and the last entry in each header is the number of days with a signal.}
\label{fig:deciles}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Models rank stocks with some skill, but the stocks they buy are not the ones that earn the most.} Opus, Sonnet, Gemini, DeepSeek, Kimi, and GLM show a hump (Figure~\ref{fig:deciles}): the second or third decile earns the most, 24--33 bp per day, and returns fall toward the top decile, so H--L lies between $-10.5$ and $+4.7$ bp. Terra and Astra rise across deciles ($+28.6$ and $+38.7$ bp), and Sol is flat. Average rank correlation does not predict profit: GLM has RankIC 0.046 and ARR $-7.8\%$, while Opus at medium effort has RankIC $-0.002$ and ARR 4.4\%, and across the 38 iterative runs the two are almost uncorrelated (Spearman 0.09). RankIC averages over the whole cross-section, but the portfolio holds only the 75 top-ranked stocks, and for most models that is where the decile profile declines.

\subsection{Case Study: Astra Learns Market Timing}
\label{sec:timing}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/fig_astra_nav_r598.png}
\caption{Cash-account NAV of the iterative Astra run at xhigh effort in 2024, against the gross equal-weight market. Shaded bands mark days on which Astra holds stocks.}
\label{fig:astra}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Astra's lead comes from staying out of the market, not from choosing better stocks.} Its program scores stocks only when the median five-session and twenty-session log returns of the universe are both positive, and holds cash otherwise. The first three candidates have no such rule. It appears in the fourth and fifth submissions, the fifth being the committed strategy, after research feedback showed net losses in both research years ($-12.2\%$ in 2022 and $-13.6\%$ in 2023). The rule keeps Astra out of the early-2024 decline (Figure~\ref{fig:astra}): it first buys stocks on 28 February, its NAV is exactly 1.000 when the market bottoms at 0.704 on 6 February, and its maximum drawdown over January and February is $-1.7\%$ against $-29.6\%$ for the market. Over the year, Astra ends at 1.107 against 1.170 for the market, $-6.3$ pp of excess return, and 12.5\% of its gain arrives in the five sessions from 24 to 30 September. Cash on 75\% of trading days caps participation: Astra captures 16\% of the market's rebound from the February low, against 73\% for Terra, so the lower drawdown comes with a lower upside. The behavior depends on effort: Astra's low, medium, and high runs are invested on nearly every day and suffer drawdowns of $-25.7\%$, $-17.9\%$, and $-26.3\%$.

\label{sec:iteration}

\noindent\textbf{Iteration raises the score for every model but the return for only four.} Pairing each model's best iterative and one-shot configurations (Table~\ref{tab:paired}), the mean score rises from 36.47 to 43.55, with gains from 0.69 (Sonnet) to 18.99 (Sol). ARR is higher in the iterative run for Sol, Astra, Kimi, and DeepSeek (by 6.2 to 16.2 pp) and lower for the other five, so the mean gain is only 2.9 pp. Feedback improves the research window more than the sealed year: the mean gain in $S_{\mathrm{in}}$ is 10.36 points against 5.67 in $S_{\mathrm{out}}$, and the iterative best configurations lose 12.4 points between the two windows against 7.7 for one-shot. Part of what iteration learns is specific to 2022--2023.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/fig_progress_r598.png}
\caption{Research-period progress of the nine iterative runs. (a)~Best-so-far excess ARR gain over the first valid candidate; means use the runs available at each index. (b)~First, best, and committed candidates on 2022--2023.}
\label{fig:progress}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Most research progress comes from repairing a weak first candidate.} Mean best-so-far gains at valid-candidate indices one to five are 0, 14.4, 16.7, 29.1, and 36.5 pp over 9, 9, 7, 4, and 3 runs (Figure~\ref{fig:progress}), but the median gain is 6.5 pp at the second candidate and 7.0 pp at the end of the run. Two runs drive the mean: DeepSeek and Sol improve by 57.1 and 50.7 pp from first candidates near $-44$ and $-45$ pp excess ARR, and without them the mean final gain is 6.5 pp. The second candidate is the research-period best in four of nine runs, and three runs commit their best candidate. Large gains come from fixing a clear error in a poor first candidate, and once candidates are near break-even, further evaluations add only a few points.

\subsection{Trading Styles}
\label{sec:style}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=.9\linewidth]{Figures/fig_styles_r598.png}
\caption{Holding-value-weighted style exposure of the nine models' best iterative configurations in 2024, in cross-sectional standard deviations relative to the market. Astra's estimates use its 61 invested days.}
\label{fig:styles}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Most models converge on small-cap, low-volatility, value-tilted holdings, and style does not separate winners from losers.} Eight of nine models tilt toward smaller stocks, seven toward higher book-to-price, and most toward lower volatility and turnover (Figure~\ref{fig:styles}). Opus and DeepSeek are the exceptions, leaning toward higher volatility ($+0.54$ and $+0.46$) and turnover ($+0.56$ and $+0.57$), and Terra stays within 0.11 of the market on every dimension. Earnings-to-price is negative for six models, so the value tilt comes from book value rather than earnings. Similar tilts end in different places: Astra (book-to-price $+0.96$, volatility $-1.05$) finishes at 1.11 and Sonnet ($+0.54$, $-0.64$) at 0.88. Account outcomes are set by market exposure and by the head of each ranking, as the previous sections show, and the style tilt adds little to them.

\subsection{Kepler-1 Against the Models}
\label{sec:kepler}

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{Figures/fig_nav_r598.png}
\caption{Cash-account NAV in 2024 for the nine models' best iterative configurations and Kepler-1, against the gross equal-weight market.}
\label{fig:nav}
\end{figure}

\noindent\textbf{Kepler-1 turns its edge into account value, while the best model barely finishes above its starting capital.} Kepler-1 scores 77.56 and ends 2024 at 1.93 against 1.17 for the market (Figure~\ref{fig:nav}), and four models finish above their starting capital: Astra (1.11), Sol (1.09), Terra (1.08), and Opus (1.04). The other five end between 0.88 (Sonnet) and 0.97 (Gemini). The lead does not come from a better average rank signal: Kepler-1's RankIC of 0.085 is below Astra's 0.101. It comes from converting signal into return, with a Sharpe ratio of 2.98 against 1.89 for the best model.
