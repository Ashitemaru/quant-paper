\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Quantitative research is an iterative optimization problem. A researcher proposes a market hypothesis, translates it into an executable signal, evaluates it on historical data, and decides which ideas to keep, revise, or abandon. The difficulty is not producing a candidate but deciding which evidence to trust, because the same loop chooses the candidate, reads the feedback, and plans the next experiment. The real output of research is therefore a frozen strategy that keeps working after the loop closes. Large language models (LLMs) are a natural fit for this loop: they combine domain reasoning, code generation, empirical diagnosis, and candidate selection. Recent alpha-discovery systems already exploit these abilities through interactive generation and multi-stage search \citep{Wang2023AlphaGPT,Tang2025AlphaAgent,Han2026QuantaAlpha}.

Evaluating this ability is hard because a research result is produced jointly by the model and the process around it. The data available, the information cutoff, the feedback during research, the number of trials, and the rule that picks the reported candidate all shape the final strategy. On historical market data, repeated backtests invite selection bias and backtest overfitting \citep{Bailey2017Backtest}. A strong number reported after an open-ended search therefore mixes signal quality with search breadth and with the choice of which trial to show.

Existing financial benchmarks measure important but narrower slices of the problem. Code-oriented suites test whether a strategy executes or can be repaired, and sequential-trading benchmarks test repeated decisions. Alpha-mining benchmarks evaluate formulas or particular search procedures \citep{AlphaQTBench2026,StockBench2025,QuantBench2025,Luo2026AlphaBench,Zhang2026AlphaForgeBench}. None of them makes the complete research run the unit of comparison: a fixed information boundary, bounded feedback, one committed strategy, and an assessment on data the model never saw.

We introduce \textbf{\BenchmarkName}, a benchmark and public leaderboard for end-to-end quantitative research by LLMs (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}).
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Research.} A model receives real Shenzhen A-share market data and a research budget, and may observe only 2022--2023. It writes Python strategies that score stocks every day, receives feedback on them, and finally freezes one strategy.
    \item \textbf{Sealed assessment.} The platform replays the frozen strategy exactly once on the sealed year 2024. It books the strategy on a cash account with real lot sizes, price-limit rules, and order-dependent trading costs.
    \item \textbf{Scoring.} The final score combines signal quality, excess return over the market, token efficiency, and the diversity of the features the strategy actually uses.
    \item \textbf{Tracks.} Two tracks separate what the model can do on its own from what it gains by iterating. \emph{One-shot} freezes a single proposal without performance feedback. \emph{Iterative research} lets the model revise against feedback within the budget. Participants can also enter with their own agent harness.
\end{itemize}

We evaluate six frontier models and a professional reference strategy, and find:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{Feedback matters, a lot.} Every model improves from one-shot to iterative research. No one-shot strategy beats the equal-weight market in 2024. The best iterative strategy (\ModelClaudeOpus) earns a 30.4\% annual return and beats the market by 13.3 percentage points.
    \item \textbf{Models remain far from professional quality.} The reference strategy scores 51.6 and earns 70.9\% in 2024; the best model scores 31.9.
    \item \textbf{Predictive correlation is not a portfolio.} The models with the highest research-window RankIC (\ModelGemini, \ModelGLM) produce the weakest 2024 portfolios. Ranking the whole market slightly better does not make the 75 selected stocks profitable.
    \item \textbf{Efficiency differs widely.} \ModelAstra\ reaches a competitive score with fewer than 100k tokens, while the other iterative runs use 300--420k tokens of the 500k budget.
\end{itemize}

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=2pt]
    \item \textbf{A benchmark for complete research runs.} \BenchmarkName\ fixes the data, information boundary, research budget, feedback policy, strategy commitment, and a sealed out-of-sample assessment. Models are therefore compared on the strategy they commit to, not on the best backtest they found.
    \item \textbf{A realistic financial and scoring protocol.} Strategies are booked on a cash account with execution constraints. A published in-sample formula covers signal quality, excess return, efficiency, and diversity, and an out-of-sample score is computed on the sealed year.
    \item \textbf{An open platform and leaderboard with a first evaluation.} The hosted Arena runs submissions from any model or harness under the same contract. We report six frontier models on two tracks, a reasoning-effort study, a feedback-granularity study, and an analysis of research behavior and failures.
\end{itemize}
