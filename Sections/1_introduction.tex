\section{Introduction}

Quantitative research is an iterative optimization problem. A researcher proposes a market hypothesis, translates it into an executable signal, evaluates it on historical data, and decides which ideas to keep, revise, or abandon. The hard part is not producing a candidate; it is deciding what evidence to trust when the candidate, the feedback, and the next experiment are all chosen by the same loop. The output is therefore more than a formula: it is a frozen strategy together with evidence that the strategy remains useful after the research loop closes. Large language models (LLMs) are a natural fit for this loop because they can combine domain reasoning, code generation, empirical diagnosis, and candidate selection. Recent systems for alpha discovery already explore these capabilities through interactive generation and multi-stage search \citep{Wang2023AlphaGPT,Tang2025AlphaAgent,Han2026QuantaAlpha}.

The difficulty is that a quantitative research result is produced jointly by the model and the research process around it. The data release, information cutoff, feedback available during research, number of trials, candidate-selection rule, and financial evaluator all affect the final strategy. Historical market data make this dependence especially strong: repeated backtests create opportunities for selection bias and backtest overfitting \citep{Bailey2017Backtest}. A result reported after an open-ended search can therefore mix signal quality with search breadth, feedback access, and the choice of which trial to present.

Existing financial benchmarks measure important but different slices of this problem. Code-oriented suites test whether a strategy is executable or repairable; sequential-trading benchmarks test repeated decisions; conventional quantitative benchmarks vary models, features, and seeds; and end-to-end alpha systems study particular mining procedures \citep{AlphaQTBench2026,StockBench2025,QuantBench2025,Luo2026AlphaBench,Zhang2026AlphaForgeBench}. These settings do not provide a common unit for comparing a complete research run: a model must receive a defined information boundary, use only the permitted research feedback, commit one strategy, and then face a final interval that was unavailable during research. Without this separation, a higher backtest result does not reveal whether the model found a more predictive signal, searched more extensively, or simply selected a more favorable trial.

This paper asks: can LLMs turn market observations into generalizable investment signals through autonomous quantitative research? We introduce \textbf{\BenchmarkName}, a released benchmark that makes the research run the unit of comparison. Each run registers a model identity, an interaction track, an authorized strategy program, chronological research and final windows, a resource contract, and one final strategy commitment. One-shot and vanilla tracks compare frozen proposals without performance feedback against iterative research with feedback. A separate errors-only control examines the feedback policy within iterative research. After commitment, every track is evaluated under the same final information boundary and financial protocol. The benchmark is designed to measure the complete path from hypothesis to frozen strategy, rather than a formula or a trading action in isolation.

The benchmark also keeps the evidence produced by that path separate. Cross-sectional RankIC measures predictive ordering, while portfolio returns and risk-adjusted performance describe financial outcomes. The primary financial analysis uses offline cash-account statistics, with historical additive results retained separately. Coverage, resource use, score eligibility, and failed or cancelled runs are recorded alongside these quantities. This separation prevents a strong signal from being treated as a guaranteed investment outcome and prevents a high annualized return on sparse dates from appearing as full-window evidence. The hosted evaluator enforces the registered data and execution boundary. Public materials describe evaluation dimensions and the research-window scoring rule; final-score construction and exact execution timing remain private.

Our current release uses raw-price observations for a registered Shenzhen A-share population, with 2022--2023 research and 2024 final assessment. The archive contains 106 episodes: 24 completed financial records in the main experiment and four in the feedback control. Eight of the 24 main-experiment strategies have positive cash-account returns, but only two exceed the aligned gross market reference. Astra improves with full feedback, while Gemini's higher RankIC coexists with lower portfolio returns. Eight of nine completed iterative runs improve their research-window excess metric, yet intermediate candidates lack final-window assessments. Together these observations locate an unresolved gap between progress during research and economic generalization; they do not establish a causal overfitting mechanism or a definitive model ranking.

The contributions of this work are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{A benchmark for complete quantitative research runs.} We define a common unit that includes point-in-time data access, bounded research feedback, resource accounting, final strategy commitment, and a sealed final assessment.
    \item \textbf{A financial protocol that separates evidence types.} We describe predictive quality, portfolio performance, coverage, resource efficiency, and feature diversity, with a public research-window formula and a separately administered private final score.
    \item \textbf{An auditable release and pilot.} We report campaign records, official scorecards, resource observations, and offline diagnostics for examining research outcomes. Evaluator versions identify the hosted assessment; private final-scoring details are not part of the public release.
\end{itemize}
