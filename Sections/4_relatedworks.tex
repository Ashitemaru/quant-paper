\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Formulaic Alpha Mining with Language Models}

Formulaic alpha mining searches for executable expressions that transform market observations into predictive cross-sectional signals. Published formula libraries demonstrate the interpretability and deployability of this representation \citep{Kakushadze2016Formulaic}. Automated approaches then use evolutionary search or learned generators to explore expression spaces and combine the resulting factors \citep{Zhang2020AutoAlpha,Shi2024AlphaForge}. These explicit search spaces support reproducible execution, but they are combinatorial, highly exposed to noisy backtest feedback, and often optimized around a narrow factor statistic rather than the behavior of the final portfolio.

LLMs extend this paradigm by connecting economic hypotheses, symbolic construction, and empirical diagnosis. LLMFactor uses sequential knowledge-guided prompting to extract interpretable factors from financial information \citep{Wang2024LLMFactor}; Automate Strategy Finding with LLM combines multimodal market inputs with a multi-agent portfolio workflow \citep{Kou2024AutomateStrategy}; Alpha-GPT introduces interactive LLM-based alpha generation \citep{Wang2023AlphaGPT}; AlphaAgent regularizes generation for originality, hypothesis--factor alignment, and structural simplicity \citep{Tang2025AlphaAgent}; and QuantaAlpha treats a complete mining trajectory as the unit of evolution and reuses effective sub-trajectories through mutation and crossover \citep{Han2026QuantaAlpha}. These systems establish the value of structured, multi-stage alpha discovery beyond one-shot formula generation. \BenchmarkName\ provides a released contract for evaluating the resulting model runs with executable strategy outputs, explicit resource use, and a frozen final-assessment boundary.

\subsection{Quantitative Benchmarks and Evaluation Protocols}

Existing financial infrastructure serves several different evaluation goals. Qlib standardizes AI-oriented quantitative workflows, while FinRL provides reproducible reinforcement-learning environments and trading evaluations \citep{Yang2020Qlib,Liu2020FinRL}. Financial language benchmarks such as FinBen and Finance Agent Benchmark evaluate domain understanding, generation, forecasting, and real-world research tasks \citep{Xie2024FinBen,Bigeard2025FinanceAgent}; general agent benchmarks such as AgentBench and MLAgentBench evaluate multi-turn interaction, tool use, and iterative experimentation \citep{Liu2023AgentBench,Huang2023MLAgentBench}. AlphaBench focuses specifically on formulaic alpha generation, evaluation, and search tasks \citep{Luo2026AlphaBench}, while AlphaForgeBench targets reproducible end-to-end factor and strategy design with LLMs \citep{Zhang2026AlphaForgeBench}. Recent trading-agent evaluation also emphasizes leakage control, attribution, and memory effects in historical markets \citep{Zhu2026KTDFin}.

The benchmark survey further separates evaluation units that should not be conflated. AlphaQT-Bench~\citep{AlphaQTBench2026} treats executability, causal safety, functional correctness, and structural compliance as distinct code-level outcomes, with a small number of repair rounds. StockBench~\citep{StockBench2025} evaluates repeated sequential decisions on a small stock universe and reports passive-hold references, whereas QuantBench~\citep{QuantBench2025} studies conventional quantitative models, feature sets, and training seeds rather than LLM research systems. AlphaForgeBench expands the number of generated implementations through many short tasks, and KTD-Fin separates trading-agent behavior from information access and attribution. These designs motivate our reporting of execution status, repeated financial outcomes, resource use, and information boundaries. They do not determine our number of runs, trading assumptions, or statistical power: a repeated generation on one market window is evidence about within-window variability, not a new market task.

Evaluating a quantitative benchmark also requires controlling the process that produced the final output. Repeated access to noisy backtests creates opportunities for multiple testing and selection bias \citep{Bailey2017Backtest}. \BenchmarkName\ addresses this requirement through frozen data releases, date-scoped execution, registered resource budgets, bounded interaction feedback, explicit financial conventions, and sealed assessment of one committed strategy. Its Arena platform preserves these conditions across model interactions, quantitative execution, and recoverable failures. The resulting protocol complements financial research libraries and task benchmarks with an operational account of complete LLM benchmark runs.
